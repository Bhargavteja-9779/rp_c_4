
## 4.5. What this means for a harvest decision

To see what interval validity means in practice, we used the mango data for the task the handheld instruments are bought for: deciding whether fruit have reached the 14% dry matter recommended at harvest [@ainsworth2016]. This case study was added after the batch results were known (deviation R2 in the protocol). It translates those results into decision terms and is not an independent test.

Each test fruit came from a consignment that had supplied no standards. A fruit was passed as mature if the lower limit of its 90% interval was at least 14%, and referred for re-measurement if the interval contained 14%. The reference was the simplest rule, passing every fruit whose predicted value is at least 14%. We report slope/bias correction, which predicted these fruit with an RMSEP of {{r_app_rmsep_sbc}}% dry matter (Table 5, Fig. 6). PDS between the handheld units was not usable for this purpose (RMSEP {{r_app_rmsep_pds}}%), and its results are in Supplementary Table S14. About {{r_app_immature_share}} of the test fruit were below 14%.

Deciding on the predicted value alone passed {{r_app_point_imm_K10}} of the immature fruit (range over settings {{r_app_point_imm_K10_range}}). Intervals taken over from the original calibration reduced this to {{r_app_astm_imm_K10}}. Intervals rebuilt from the reference fruit reduced it further, and here the sampling of the reference fruit mattered more than the formula. With reference fruit from two consignments, TJ+N passed {{r_app_tjn_imm_K2}} of the immature fruit and the Gaussian interval {{r_app_gauss_imm_K2}}. With the same number spread over ten consignments, the rates were {{r_app_tjn_imm_K10}} and {{r_app_gauss_imm_K10}}. The two interval formulas behaved almost identically at this level, so the case study does not favour TJ+N over a Gaussian interval. What it shows is the cost of drawing the reference fruit from too few consignments: {{r_app_ratio_tjn}} times as many immature fruit passed with TJ+N. The leave-one-consignment-out TJ+B passed {{r_app_tjb_imm_K10}} with ten consignments. With two consignments it referred every fruit, because a bounded 90% interval requires at least nine consignments.

The price of these decisions is the share of fruit that needs a second look: {{r_app_tjn_ref_K10}} with TJ+N and {{r_app_tjb_ref_K10}} with TJ+B, for ten consignments. A simpler alternative is a fixed guard band around the threshold, for example passing fruit only if the prediction exceeds 14% by one RMSE. Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table S16 show the whole trade-off between the immature fruit passed and the fruit referred, as the guard band is widened. The interval rules lie on these curves, as they must: a 90% Gaussian interval is by construction a guard band of *t* × RMSE (about {{r14b_gauss_equiv_k}}), and TJ+N sits next to it. At the same re-measurement rate, a guard band did as well as or slightly better than TJ+N (with ten consignments, {{r14b_k10_guard_at_tjn}} against {{r_app_tjn_imm_K10}} of immature fruit passed at {{r14b_k10_tjn_ref}} referred). Intervals therefore do not beat a well-chosen guard band. What they add is a principled choice of the band from a stated coverage. That choice holds only when the reference fruit come from enough consignments; with two consignments the stated coverage was not met. The sampling of the reference fruit, by contrast, moved the whole curve. At the re-measurement rate of TJ+N with two consignments ({{r14b_ref}} of fruit referred), guard bands passed {{r14b_imm_k2}} of the immature fruit when the reference fruit came from two consignments and {{r14b_imm_k10}} when they came from ten. Neither intervals nor guard bands can make a single NIR reading more precise; for fruit close to the threshold, a second measurement is the only remedy.
