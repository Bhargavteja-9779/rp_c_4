
A laboratory that already has the left-out errors of its standards will naturally try a simpler formula first. We therefore compared TJ+N on identical standards with five such formulas (Table 3; {{r_e11_n}} scenario-pipeline combinations, 200 repetitions each): a *t*-interval from the leave-one-out RMSE (Gauss-LOO), with and without σ(x) scaling; the ASTM formula with the standard error re-estimated on the standards (ASTM-T); for slope/bias correction, the textbook prediction interval of that regression (OLS-PI); and a bootstrap interval (Boot-T).

At 90% most of them did well. Gauss-LOO was too narrow in {{r_gauss_under_010}} combinations, ASTM-T in {{r_astmt_under_010}}, and OLS-PI in {{r_ols_under_010}} slope/bias cases. They were also often narrower. Where both were valid, OLS-PI had the lower interval score in {{r_ols_better}} slope/bias scenarios and TJ+N in {{r_ols_worse}}. The scaled Gaussian interval had the lower score in {{r_gaussn_better}} (see also Supplementary Table S7). For 90% statements with randomly chosen standards, a Gaussian interval from the left-out errors is therefore a reasonable shortcut, and for slope/bias correction the textbook regression interval is usually the better choice.

At 95% the comparison needs care. TJ+N cannot give a bounded 95% interval with fewer than 19 standards; it was unbounded in {{r_e11_tjn_unb_005}} combinations here (and in {{r_e1_tjn_unb_005}} of the main scenarios), so "never too narrow" is partly automatic. Restricted to $m\ge20$, where TJ+N was bounded, it was too narrow in {{r_tjn_under_005_m20}} combinations, against {{r_gauss_under_005_m20}} for Gauss-LOO, {{r_astmt_under_005_m20}} for ASTM-T and {{r_ols_under_005_m20}} for OLS-PI. The shortfalls of the formulas were small, though: with $m\ge20$, flagged intervals still covered at least {{r_flag_min_cov_m20}} of the samples (nominal 0.95). Using the same 0.02 margin as for TJ+N, and again $m\ge20$, Gauss-LOO was within 0.02 of nominal in {{r_ni_gauss_005_m20}} combinations, ASTM-T in {{r_ni_astmt_005_m20}} and OLS-PI in {{r_ni_ols_005_m20}}. The practical difference at 95% is therefore a few percentage points of coverage at most, not a failure of the simpler methods.

The bootstrap behaved differently. As implemented here (pairs resampling of the standards plus resampled in-sample residuals), it was too narrow in {{r_boot_under_010}} combinations at 90%, including {{r_boot_under_m10}} with ten standards. With 10–20 standards, the in-sample residuals understate the error of a new sample. A bootstrap based on leverage-corrected or out-of-sample residuals may do better; we did not test one.

The coverage shortfall of the Gaussian formulas was associated with the shape of the errors. For Gauss-LOO it grew with the skewness of the prediction errors (Spearman ρ = {{r_e12_gauss_skew}}) and with the ratio of the actual error to the leave-one-out RMSE (ρ = {{r_e12_gauss_ratio}}). For OLS-PI it grew with skewness (ρ = {{r_e12_ols_skew}}) and with excess kurtosis (ρ = {{r_e12_ols_kurt}}). A *t*-interval sets its 95% limits from the standard deviation and a normal tail shape, so skewed or heavy-tailed errors are under-covered in the longer tail. TJ+N takes its limits from the empirical distribution of the absolute left-out errors, so it does not rely on a normal tail shape. With 20–50 standards it is also conservative by construction, because the 95% limit is then set by the largest or second-largest left-out error.
