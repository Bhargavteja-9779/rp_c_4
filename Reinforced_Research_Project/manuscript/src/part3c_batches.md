
## 4.3. Where the standards come from matters more than how many there are

The results so far assume that the standards are a random sample of everything the instrument will measure later. In a packhouse this is rarely true. The fruit used to adapt a new handheld unit are typically picked from one or two consignments on the day of installation, and the unit is then used on consignments that arrive weeks later. We reproduced this on the mango data, which record each fruit's consignment, in eight setting-pipeline combinations: the season shift with SBC and without transfer, and the three handheld-instrument pairs with PDS and SBC. These eight combinations are not independent. They come from one product, the instrument pairs share the master unit and some consignments, and the two pipelines of a pair share their standards. The errors of the transferred predictors were clearly correlated within consignments: the intra-consignment correlation averaged {{r_icc_range}} over the combinations.

Table 4, Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table S11 show the coverage for fruit from consignments that supplied no standards. With 20 standards taken from only two consignments, TJ+N was too narrow in {{r_k2_tjn_under}} combinations, with coverage between {{r_k2_tjn_cov}} for a nominal 0.90. Several alternatives failed as well. Gaussian intervals from the same left-out errors were too narrow in {{r_k2_gauss_under}} combinations, the split-standards interval in {{r_k2_split_under}}, CV+ with consignments as folds but every fruit counted as a separate unit in {{r_k2_lgo_under}}, and the intervals carried over from the source calibration in all of them.

The shortfall was largest for slope/bias correction (Supplementary Table S11). A plausible reason is that a slope and offset fitted on two consignments absorb part of those consignments' own bias, so their left-out errors underestimate how different the next consignment will be. For the remaining fruit of the two sampled consignments, the same TJ+N intervals were accurate (coverage {{r_k2_tjn_same_cov}}).

Spreading the 20 standards over more consignments reduced the problem. TJ+N was too narrow in {{r_tjn_under_K5}} combinations with 5 × 4 standards, and in {{r_tjn_under_K10}} with 10 × 2. With one fruit from each of 20 consignments, available for the instrument pairs only, it was too narrow in none ({{r_tjn_under_K20}}), as Appendix B predicts. The 15 × 1 design, the only one-per-consignment design possible for the season shift, uses 15 standards. It also showed no under-coverage ({{r_tjn_under_K15}}), but its coverage of {{r_tjn_cov_K15}} is inflated by the rank rule, because with 15 standards the 90% limit is the largest left-out error. We had expected the intra-consignment correlation to explain which combinations failed. It did not (Spearman ρ = {{r_h10d_rho}} across the eight combinations with two consignments; Supplementary Fig. S6): the correlation was high everywhere, and the number of consignments decided the outcome.

The methods that treat the consignment as the unit held their level. TJ+B was never too narrow where it gave a bounded interval ({{r_tjb_under_a01}} combinations at 90%, {{r_tjb_under_a02}} at 80%, {{r_tjb_under_a005}} at 95%; coverage {{r_tjb_cov}} at 90%). With fewer than nine consignments it gives no finite 90% interval. With 10 × 2 standards its intervals were wider than those of TJ+N (median width ratio {{r_tjb_width_10}}). With one fruit per consignment the two methods are identical. In the designs studied here, the max-score variant TJ+G gave exactly the same intervals as TJ+B.

The simplest batch-aware option worked as well. A *t*-interval built from leave-one-consignment-out errors, with K − 1 degrees of freedom (Gauss-LGO), was too narrow in {{r_gausslgo_under_a01}} of the random-design combinations at 90%. It has no distribution-free guarantee, and with two consignments its *t* factor made it {{r_gausslgo_width_2}} times wider than TJ+N. From five consignments onwards its width was reasonable (width ratio to TJ+N {{r_gausslgo_ratio_5}} with 5 × 4, {{r_gausslgo_ratio_10}} with 10 × 2). With 5 × 4 standards it was the only interval in the comparison that was both bounded and never too narrow. The classical random-effects interval (ANOVA-PI), computed from the same leave-one-consignment-out errors, was too narrow in {{r_anova_under_K2}} combinations with 2 × 10 standards, {{r_anova_under_K5}} with 5 × 4 and {{r_anova_under_K10}} with 10 × 2. The common message of these results is that the standards must be left out *consignment by consignment* when the errors are estimated. Which formula is then applied to those errors matters less, as long as it accounts for the small number of consignments.

The chronological design is the least favourable case. Its standards came from the five earliest consignments, and it was tested on everything harvested later: weeks later in the season setting, and in later seasons for the instrument pairs, partly from other regions and cultivars. TJ+N was too narrow in {{r_chrono_tjn_under}} combinations, Gauss-LOO in {{r_chrono_gauss_under}}, ANOVA-PI in {{r_chrono_anova_under}}, Gauss-LGO in {{r_chrono_gausslgo_under}} and the source-based ASTM interval in {{r_chrono_astm_under}}. TJ+B could not give a bounded 90% interval from five consignments. At the 80% level, where five consignments are enough, it covered {{r_chrono_tjb_020}} of the later fruit. This design uses a single, fixed set of consignments per setting, so it is one case study rather than a general result. No guarantee covers standards taken only from the start of a season.

For a laboratory the conclusion is cheap to act on. The cost of a transfer lies in the reference analyses, and those cost the same whether 20 fruit come from 2 consignments or from 20. In these data, taking one or two samples from each of many consignments gave intervals that remained valid for new consignments, while taking many samples from a few did not. We expect the same for other products that arrive in lots, but we could not test this.
